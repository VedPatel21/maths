G0

The mathematical language you need first

No prior statistics is assumed. An observation is one recorded value. A data set is a collection of observations. If five observations are 3, 5, 4, 5, 8, the first is x1 = 3 and the fifth is x5 = 8. The subscript is an address, not multiplication. n = 5 tells us how many observations there are.

A fraction a/b means a divided by b. Probability 0.2 is 2/10 = 1/5 = 20%. To turn a decimal into a percentage multiply by 100. To turn a percentage into a probability divide by 100. Thus 35% = 0.35, not 35.

A square means multiply a number by itself: 4² = 16; (-4)² = 16. Parentheses matter: -4² without parentheses is conventionally -(4²) = -16. A square root reverses a nonnegative square: √16 = 4. Keep negative observations inside parentheses when squaring them.

Summation means addition. The symbol Σ says to add the indicated terms. In L2 p. 17 the data are 3, 5, 4, 5, 8:

Σx = 3+5+4+5+8 = 25; Σx² = 9+25+16+25+64 = 139; (Σx)² = 25² = 625.

Σx² means square first, then add. (Σx)² means add first, then square. These are different and the distinction appears in variance calculations.

A mean uses all observations. x-bar (usually written x̄) is a sample mean; µ, pronounced mu, is a population mean. s² and s are sample variance and SD; σ² and σ, pronounced sigma, are population variance and SD. The superscript 2 marks a square, not a new unrelated variable. n is sample size, N is population size, although a probability question may use N for a count of possible objects. Always read the local definition.

Inequalities: x < 5 excludes 5; x ≤ 5 includes it. For an integer count, x ≥ 3 means 3,4,5, ...; x > 3 means 4,5, ... . "At least" means ≥, "at most" means ≤, "exactly" means =. For integer x, x ≥ 3 is the complement of x ≤ 2.

Sets: {1,3,5} is a list of permitted outcomes. ∅ is the empty set. S is the sample space. A ⊆ B says every outcome in A is also in B. ∪ means OR, ∩ means AND, and Ac means NOT A. The vertical bar in P(A|B) reads "given B". P stands for probability, a number from 0 to 1. E(x) is expected value; Var(x) is variance. L9 uses lowercase x for a random variable, and this guide follows that convention.

Calculator order: work inside parentheses, evaluate powers, then multiplication/division, then addition/subtraction. For [793-51²/13]/12, enter (793-(51*51)/13)/12. Keep extra digits until the final answer. A sensible final approximation is often 4 decimal places for probability and 3-4 for a computed SD; follow any instructions on the actual paper.

G1

What statistics is, and what kind of data you have

Source: L1 pp. 3-14; L2 pp. 1,10. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Statistics learns from data. It begins by identifying the object studied, the characteristic measured and the group about which a conclusion is needed. A single fish is an experimental or observational unit; its length is a variable. A variable is a characteristic that can differ between units.

B. Why do we need it?

We cannot usually measure everyone. A sample lets us study a manageable group, but conclusions about a larger group have uncertainty. This is why a statement about a sample and a statement estimating a population are different even when both use the same number.

C. Core definitions

A population is the complete group of interest. A sample is a subset of that population. A census measures every unit in the population. A statistic summarizes a sample; a parameter summarizes a population. Descriptive statistics organizes and summarizes the observed data. Inferential statistics uses sample information to estimate, predict or generalize about the population. A measure of reliability describes uncertainty in that generalization.

Quantitative data use a meaningful numerical measurement scale: length, weight, age, number of children. Qualitative data describe categories: species, color, computer type. Numeric labels do not turn categories into quantities: averaging jersey numbers would not give a meaningful measure of players.

The notes list four collection methods: a published source already contains the data; a designed experiment imposes treatments; a survey asks questions; an observational study records units in their setting. A simple random sample gives every possible subset of a fixed size the same chance. A representative sample reflects relevant population characteristics; random selection helps but does not guarantee perfect representation in one sample.

D. Method and recognition clues

There is no calculation formula here. Ask: Who is the target group? Who was actually measured? Is the statement only about those observations, or about the larger group? "Estimate all students using 50 surveyed students" signals inference. "Among these 50, the median is 20" is descriptive. "Reference book" signals a published source, even if its original researchers used a survey.

E. Guided source example: the 144 fish

1
L1 p. 11 records location, species, length, weight and DDT concentration for 144 fish.
2
Given: one row per captured fish.
3
Need: classify variables. Location and species identify groups, so are qualitative. Length in centimeters, weight in grams and concentration in parts per million are meaningful numerical measurements, so are quantitative. The unit is one fish, not a creek. The 144 fish are the sample; the target fish population must be defined by the study's scope rather than invented from the sample size.

F. New exam-style example

A researcher surveys 40 Lakehead students and uses their average commuting time, 18 minutes, to estimate the average for all Lakehead students. Calling 18 minutes an estimate for the whole university is inferential: it goes beyond the measured students. Reporting that the sample average is 18 minutes is descriptive. The arithmetic can be identical; the claim's target determines its type.

G. Common mistakes

Do not classify a study solely because it contains a mean. Do not confuse random selection with asking whichever friends are available. A census removes sampling uncertainty but can still have recording errors. The source's history of statistics (L1 p. 2) provides context, not a substitute for these distinctions.

H. Independent practice

  1. A hospital records blood type and body temperature. Classify each variable.
  2. An article reports salaries from a government report. Which collection method is used by the article's author?
  3. A sample of 30 workers has median age 29; the author says all company workers probably have median age near 29. Identify the descriptive and inferential parts.
Show answers · I. Worked answers
  1. Blood type is qualitative because it is a category. Temperature is quantitative because numerical differences on its scale have meaning.
  2. Published source: the author obtained data already collected and published. The government's original collection method is a separate question.
  3. "The 30 sampled workers have median 29" describes observed data. Extending that result to all company workers is an inference with sampling uncertainty.

J. Fast revision

Remember: unit = one object; population = all targets; sample = measured subset; statistic estimates parameter. Describe the observed group or infer about a larger one. Categories can have numeric codes. A simple random sample equalizes probabilities for whole same-size samples.

G2

Frequencies and graphical displays

Source: L2 pp. 2-16; image-only charts included. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

A frequency counts how often a class occurs. A display turns many observations into a pattern. Categories need category displays; numerical measurements need numerical displays.

B. Why do we need it?

A list of 500 computer buyers is hard to compare. Three counts, proportions and a picture immediately reveal which category is largest. Correct graph choice is an inexpensive source of conceptual marks.

C. Definitions

A class is a category or numerical interval. Class frequency f is its count. Relative frequency is the fraction of all n observations in that class. A bar chart compares categories with separated bars; a Pareto chart orders those bars from largest to smallest. A pie chart partitions a circle into proportional slices.

A dot plot puts one dot per observation above a numerical axis and stacks repeats. A stem-and-leaf display preserves individual values using a key. A histogram groups a numerical variable into adjacent intervals; its horizontal axis is a numerical scale rather than unrelated category names. In the equal-width histograms used here, height represents frequency or relative frequency. With unequal widths, area needs care; that extension is not needed for these notes.

D. Formulas and selection

Relative frequency = f/n; percentage = 100(f/n)%; pie angle = 360°(f/n).

Here f is the class count and n is the total count. Frequencies sum to n, relative frequencies to 1, percentages to 100 and pie angles to 360°. Use 360, not 100, when asked for degrees. For an initial equal-width histogram, class width is approximately (maximum-minimum)/(number of classes); choose sensible boundaries that include all observations. [a, b) includes a but excludes b so adjacent classes do not double-count a boundary.

E. Guided source example

1
L2 p. 3 lists A C B B D B C B B A. Count rather than guess: A occurs 2 times, B 5, C 2, D 1.
2
Total n = 10.
3
For A: f/n = 2/10 = 0.2, percentage = 20%, angle = 0.2×360° = 72°. Similarly B is 0.5, 50%, 180°; C is 0.2, 20%, 72°; D is 0.1, 10%, 36°. Angles total 360°. A Pareto chart places B first, A/C next, D last. Ties may be ordered either way.

The iMac table (L2 pp. 5-9) has counts 85,60,355 out of 500, giving 17%,12%,71%. Its pie and bar charts express that same categorical information.

F. New exam-style example

1
A numerical list is 0,0.5,1.0,1.4,1.5.
2
For classes [0,0.5), [0.5,1), [1,1.5), [1.5,2), the frequencies are 1,1,2,1. The value 0.5 belongs only to the second class. Use a histogram for the class intervals, not a category pie chart if the goal is to show the numerical shape.

G. Common mistakes

A boxplot, histogram and stem-leaf display describe quantitative data in this course. Do not confuse a histogram's interval with a bar chart's category. Never interpret a stem without its key: 6|3 may mean 63 years, whereas 3|2 may mean GPA 3.2 with a different key. Image-only L2 p. 15 explicitly gives 6|3 = 63 years.

H. Independent practice

  2. With key 2|7 = 2.7, interpret stems 1|4 8 and 2|0 7.
  3. Which display orders categories by frequency? Which numerical display preserves every original value?
Show answers · I. Worked answers
  1. 8/40 = 0.2; 0.2×100 = 20%; 0.2×360° = 72°.
  2. Read the key's decimal position: 1.4,1.8,2.0,2.7. There are four observations, not two.
  3. Pareto chart. A stem-and-leaf display preserves values when supplied with a key; a dot plot also preserves values on its scale. A histogram generally loses exact within-interval values.

J. Fast revision

Remember: frequency/count, proportion/divide by n, percentage/×100, degrees/×360. Histogram = numerical intervals; bar/Pareto/pie = categories. Source puppy intervals (L2 p. 13) count 4,5,0,1, so a zero-frequency class must still be represented on the numerical axis.

G3

Mean, median, mode and skewness

MeanAdd every observation and divide by the count. The mean uses every value.
MedianSort first, then locate the middle. The median is more resistant to extremes.

Source: L2 pp. 17-26. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

A measure of center describes a typical value. The mean is a balance point, the median is a middle position and the mode is a most frequent value. They answer different questions.

B. Why do we need it?

A single extremely high salary can pull an average upward while most salaries stay low. Comparing mean and median explains why a typical person's experience may differ from the reported average.

C. Definitions

The sample mean is x-bar; population mean is µ. To find a median, sort values smallest to largest first. With odd n the median is the observation at position (n+1)/2. With even n it is the average of positions n/2 and n/2+1. A mode is a repeated most frequent value. Following L2 p. 21, if no values repeat, report no mode; two tied repeated peaks are bimodal.

D. Formulas and recognition

x¯=ΣxnSample mean: add the observations, then divide by n.
x̄ = (Σx)/n; µ = (Σx)/N.

x is each observation, Σ means add, and n or N is the number of observations in the sample or population. Mean uses all values; median uses ordered positions; mode uses counts. A right-skewed distribution has a long right tail and commonly mean > median. A left tail commonly pulls mean below median. Symmetric data balance around a center; mean and median coincide in the source's symmetric examples. Treat the skew comparisons as practical patterns, not universal proofs for irregular distributions.

E. Guided source example

1
L2 pp. 19-21 uses 5,3,4,5,8.
2
Given n = 5.
3
Need three centers.
4
For mean, add: 5+3+4+5+8 = 25, then divide: 25/5 = 5.
5
For median, order: 3,4,5,5,8. Position (5+1)/2 = 3, so median is the third value 5.
6
For mode, 5 occurs twice while others occur once, so mode = 5. Equal answers here do not make the methods interchangeable.

F. New exam-style example

1
For 1,2,3,4,30, mean = (1+2+3+4+30)/5 = 40/5 = 8. The middle ordered value is 3. None repeats, so no mode. The extreme 30 pulls mean above median; the long tail is on the right.
2
Median is more resistant to this outlier. "Resistant" means it is less affected, not that it can never change.

G. Common mistakes

Do not find the middle of an unsorted list. Do not use (maximum+minimum)/2 as the median. Even-n medians can be absent from the observed values. A numerical mode is a value, not its frequency. L2 p. 25's left-skew mean is corrected to 520.2, still less than median 600.

H. Independent practice

  1. Find mean, median and mode of 2,7,3,7,6.
  2. Find median of 2,3,6,9.
  3. Replace 9 in the second list by 90. What happens to its mean and median?
Show answers · I. Worked answers
  1. Sum = 25, n = 5, mean = 25/5 = 5. Sorted list: 2,3,6,7,7, median = 6. The value 7 repeats most, so mode = 7.
  2. Middle positions are 2 and 3; median = (3+6)/2 = 4.5.
  3. Old mean = 20/4 = 5; new mean = (2+3+6+90)/4 = 101/4 = 25.25. Middle values remain 3 and 6, so median remains 4.5. This illustrates resistance.

J. Fast revision

Remember: mean/add-divide; median/sort-middle; mode/count. Inference concerns how a statistic is used, not which center you computed. If you cannot explain why the middle positions are correct, write their indices above the sorted list.

G4

Range, sample variance, population variance and SD

Sample varianceUse s² and divide squared deviations by n−1.
Population varianceUse σ² and divide by the entire population size N.

Source: L3 pp. 1-11. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Spread measures how far values lie from one another or from their mean. Two samples can both average 3, while one is tightly packed and the other is widely dispersed.

B. Why do we need it?

A stable salary or reliable delivery time requires more than knowing the average. A measure of spread quantifies consistency. SD is especially useful because it has the original measurement units.

C. Definitions

Range = maximum-minimum. A deviation is x-mean. Deviations sum to zero, so simply averaging signed deviations would hide spread. Variance squares them before averaging. Sample variance divides by n-1, accounting for estimating the mean from that sample. Once n-1 deviations are known, the last is constrained by their sum, which motivates the lost degree of freedom. Population variance averages over all N units and divides by N. SD is the nonnegative square root of variance; it can be zero.

D. Formulas and assumptions

s2=Σ(x−x¯)2n−1Sample variance: divide the sum of squared deviations by n−1.
s² = Σ(x-x̄)²/(n-1) = [Σx²-(Σx)²/n]/(n-1); s = √s².
σ² = Σ(x-µ)²/N = (Σx²)/N-µ²; σ = √σ².

Use s² for a sample, n ≥ 2; σ² for the entire stated population. Σx² means sum of squared observations; (Σx)² means square of the sum. The subtraction in the shortcut produces the sum of squared deviations, not yet the variance. If lengths are centimeters, variance is cm² and SD is cm. Both are nonnegative; negative variance signals an error or extreme rounding.

E. Guided source example

1
L3 pp. 6-7 compares 1,2,3,4,5 with 2,3,3,3,4.
2
For the first, n = 5 and x-bar = 15/5 = 3. Deviations: -2, -1,0,1,2.
3
Squares: 4,1,0,1,4.
4
Sum = 10.
5
Hence s² = 10/(5-1) = 2.5 and s = √2.5 ≈ 1.5811.
6
Shortcut check: Σx² = 55; [55-15²/5]/4 = (55-45)/4 = 2.5.

For the second sample, mean is also 3 but squared deviations are 1,0,0,0,1. s² = 2/4 = 0.5; s ≈ 0.7071. The second sample is less spread out despite equal means. This is the reason spread adds information.

F. New exam-style example

1
The complete population is 2,4,6. N = 3, µ = (2+4+6)/3 = 4.
2
Squared deviations are 4,0,4, summing to 8.
3
Population variance = 8/3 ≈ 2.6667, SD ≈ 1.6330. If these three measurements were instead a sample from a larger population, sample variance would be 8/(3-1) = 4, and sample SD = 2. The wording chooses the denominator.

G. Common mistakes

Do not use n when the question says sample. Do not forget to take a square root when asked for SD. Do not square a negative number without parentheses. Do not round the mean so early that squared deviations become inaccurate. For original sample III1, keeping sum and sum of squares exact avoids this problem.

  2. For that same list treated as a complete population, find variance.
  3. All sample observations are 8. What is its SD, and can you compute an ordinary z-score?

  3. Every deviation is zero, so variance and SD are zero. z = (x-mean)/SD would divide by zero, so it is undefined. The data have no spread.

J. Fast revision

Remember this for the exam: write n, Σx and Σx² before using the shortcut. Sample divides by n-1; population divides by N; SD takes the square root. Keep full precision until the last line. Original III1 in Document 4 is the main rehearsal for this workflow.

G5

Chebyshev's rule and the empirical rule

Source: L3 pp. 12-21; L4 pp. 9-11; L10 pp. 5-6. Priority: empirical Must; Chebyshev Should.

A. What is it?

Both rules describe how much data lie near the mean. "Within two SDs" means go two SDs down and two SDs up from the mean. The total interval width is four SDs, not two.

B. Why do we need it?

A mean and SD can answer approximate percentage and percentile questions without listing every observation. The question's distribution-shape information decides which rule is justified.

C. Definitions

Chebyshev's rule gives a guaranteed minimum proportion for any shape, assuming finite variance. The empirical rule gives approximate proportions for a normally distributed or approximately mound-shaped symmetric distribution. A lower bound is not an exact percentage. "At least 75%" permits 80%,90% or 100%.

D. Formulas, symbols and conditions

Chebyshev: proportion within mean ± k(SD) is at least 1-1/k², for k > 1.
Empirical: within 1 SD ≈ 68%; within 2 SD ≈ 95%; within 3 SD ≈ 99.7%.

k is the number of SDs from the mean to an endpoint. Given a symmetric interval [a, b], check its midpoint is the mean and use k = (b-mean)/SD. Chebyshev applies regardless of skewness. The ordinary standardized form assumes SD > 0; k = 1 only gives the uninformative bound zero. For a normal-shaped distribution, symmetry splits outside probability equally between tails: below µ-σ is (100-68)/2 = 16%; below µ-2σ is 2.5%; below µ-3σ is 0.15%.

Between the mean and one SD above it is about 34%; between one and two SDs on one side is (95-68)/2 = 13.5%; between two and three is (99.7-95)/2 = 2.35%. These are the coarse empirical-rule approximations used in the text, so do not mix them with more precise diagram percentages.

E. Guided source examples

L3 p. 16 has mean 72, SD 5, 95 students. Interval (62,82) is 72 ± 10 = 72 ± 2×5. With no normal-shape assumption, Chebyshev gives 1-1/2² = 1-1/4 = 75% minimum. Interval (57,87) is ±3 SDs, so minimum fraction = 1-1/9 = 8/9. Multiply by 95: 95×8/9 = 84.444... . A count must be a whole number; at least 84.444 means at least 85 students, not 84.

1
L3 p. 21 gives normal-shaped kitten weights with µ = 3.6 lb, σ = 0.4 lb.
2

F. New exam-style example

A normal-shaped distribution has mean 50, SD 4. Its 16th percentile is about 50-4 = 46. Reason: 68% is within one SD, leaving 32% outside, half of which lies below the lower endpoint. If the question only says "unknown shape, " 46 is not justified as the 16th percentile.

G. Common mistakes

Do not apply 68/95/99.7 to every dataset. Do not call a Chebyshev minimum an equality. For a minimum number, round a noninteger lower bound upward. Inverse Chebyshev: a desired fraction c requires k = 1/√(1-c). For 95%, k = √20 ≈ 4.4721; the source p. 14 row has a numerical typo.

H. Independent practice

  1. Any-shaped data have mean 30, SD 3. Give the Chebyshev minimum within 24 to 36.
  2. Normal-shaped data have mean 100, SD 10. Approximate the proportion above 120 and the proportion from 90 to 120.
  3. For any shape, which k gives a minimum proportion of 0.96?
Show answers · I. Worked answers
  1. Distance from mean = 6 = 2×3, so k = 2. Bound = 1-1/4 = 0.75.
  2. 120 is +2 SDs; upper tail is (1-0.95)/2 = 0.025. From -1 to +2, exclude the 16% left tail and 2.5% right tail: 100-16-2.5 = 81.5%.
  3. Solve 1-1/k² = 0.96: 1/k² = 0.04; k² = 25; positive k = 5.

J. Fast revision

Remember: identify shape, convert distances to SDs, draw the interval, then choose lower bound or approximate area. Chebyshev: 2→75%, 3→88.89%, 4→93.75%. Normal tails: -1→16%, -2→2.5%, -3→0.15%.

G6

Percentiles, quartiles, z-scores and outliers

Source: L4 pp. 1-21. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Relative standing says where a value lies compared with others. A percentile uses rank; a z-score uses distance from the mean in SD units. An outlier is unusually far from the rest of the data.

B. Why do we need it?

Scoring 80 on a difficult test can be more impressive than 85 on an easy one. Quartiles also let us locate the middle half of a dataset without being dominated by extreme observations.

C. Definitions

QL is the lower quartile (25th percentile), M is the median (50th) and QU is the upper quartile (75th). IQR = QU-QL describes the middle 50%'s spread. The five-number summary is minimum, QL, median, QU, maximum. Percentage measures an amount out of 100; percentile describes a rank position. With ties and finite samples, percentile descriptions are approximate rank summaries, not necessarily exact strict percentages below a value.

D. Formulas and the instructor's convention

Percentile rank index r = (p/100)(n+1); z = (x-x̄)/s or (x-µ)/σ.

p is the desired percentile, n the number of ordered observations. If r is an integer, take that ranked observation. If r lies between consecutive ranks, this package uses their simple average, following L4 p. 6. The notes also permit weighted interpolation, so state the convention when relevant. Do not substitute a calculator's default percentile rule without checking it. Very extreme r outside the data ranks is not specified in the source.

x is the value being standardized. The mean and SD must come from the same dataset or distribution, with positive SD. A positive z is above the mean, a negative z below; |z| is distance ignoring direction. Normality is unnecessary to compute z. It is needed to attach empirical-rule percentages to z.

Inner fences: QL-1.5(IQR), QU+1.5(IQR). Outer fences: QL-3(IQR), QU+3(IQR).

Course labels (L4 pp. 19-20): distance outside the nearer quartile / IQR from 1.5 to 3 inclusive = potential outlier; >3 = outlier. z method: 2 ≤ |z| ≤ 3 potential; |z| > 3 outlier. If IQR = 0, use fence comparisons rather than a ratio dividing by zero and describe the degeneracy. A boxplot's box extends QL to QU with a median line; whiskers end at the extreme observed values inside the inner fences, and more extreme observations are separately marked.

E. Guided source examples

1
L4 pp. 5-6 sorts 15 grades as 80,80,82,84,85,86,88,90,91,92,92,94,96,98,100.
2
For QL: r = 0.25×16 = 4, so QL = 84.
3

4

For the two tests (L4 p. 12), z1 = (80-75)/2 = 2.5; z2 = (85-80)/5 = 1. The first score is farther above its class mean, so has better relative standing if higher scores are preferable.

F. New exam-style example

Given QL = 20 and QU = 28, IQR = 8. Inner fences are 20-12 = 8 and 28+12 = 40. Outer fences are 20-24 = -4 and 28+24 = 52. A value 45 lies between the upper inner and outer fences: ratio (45-28)/8 = 17/8 = 2.125, so it is potential under course terminology. A value 60 has ratio 32/8 = 4 > 3, so it is an outlier.

G. Common mistakes

Do not confuse the index r with the percentile's value. z uses mean and SD, never quartiles. Outlier detection does not prove a measurement is wrong; a real rare value can be an outlier. Never automatically delete it. The source includes boundary equality for potential labels; follow its inequalities rather than a generic textbook label.

H. Independent practice

  1. Ordered data: 1,2,4,6,8,10,13. Find QL, median, QU, IQR.
  2. A value 65 occurs in a distribution with mean 50, SD 5. Find z and its course outlier label.
  3. If QL = 10, QU = 14, classify value 25 using fences.

G7

Experiments, sample spaces and probabilities

Source: L5 pp. 1-16. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Probability measures uncertainty before an outcome is observed. An experiment is the process; an outcome is one possible result; the sample space S lists all results; an event is a selected subset of those results.

B. Why do we need it?

Before calculating a probability, we need to know what is being recorded. Two dice can yield a recorded ordered pair or a recorded sum. Those are different sample spaces, even though the physical rolls are the same.

C. Core definitions

A deterministic model repeats the same output for the same inputs. L5 p. 2 models a falling apple's height by h = 300-gt²/2, with g = 9.8. A probabilistic model includes randomness, such as a coin toss; repeated outcomes can differ but do not have to differ every time. The history in L5 p. 1 traces early probability to games of chance; the practical task here is modeling outcomes correctly.

A simple event contains one outcome. ∅ is impossible; S is certain. Finite and countably infinite spaces are discrete in the course's terminology. Repeating a coin until its first head has the countable outcomes H, TH, TTH, ... . A measured weight modeled on an interval is continuous and uncountable. Do not infer that all infinite spaces are continuous.

D. Probability rules

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1; P(S) = 1; P(∅) = 0; probabilities of distinct discrete outcomes sum to 1.
If all N outcomes are equally likely: P(A) = number in A / N.

P(A) is the sum of individual outcome probabilities in A. The counting ratio needs equal likelihood, not merely a finite list. A loaded die has unequal masses. A weather model with "rain" and "no rain" does not imply probability 1/2 simply because there are two labels.

E. Guided source example

L5 p. 12 rolls a fair die. S = {1,2,3,4,5,6}, each outcome 1/6. Let E = even = {2,4,6}; T = greater than 2 = {3,4,5,6}. Then P(E) = 1/6+1/6+1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2, and P(T) = 4/6 = 2/3. "Fair" justifies the equal masses.

F. New exam-style example

1
Two fair dice are rolled and only their sum is recorded. The recorded space is {2,3, ...,12}. The sums are not equally likely.
2

G. Common mistakes

Do not confuse event with probability: A is a set, P(A) a number. Do not count heads/tails as equally likely without a fair-coin assumption. The newborn-weight interval (0,30) on L5 p. 6 is the example's modeling range, not a universal biological claim. The first-head example assumes eventual termination; an endless-tail path has probability zero for an ordinary fair independent coin.

G8

Counting: multiplication, factorials, permutations and combinations

Permutation: order mattersUse for distinct roles or a row: P(N,n)=N!/(N−n)!
Combination: order does not matterUse for a committee or group: C(N,n)=N!/[n!(N−n)!]

Source: L5 pp. 17-18; L6 pp. 1-4. Priority: basic counting Must; full restrictions Should.

A. What is it?

Counting avoids writing a very long sample space. A multistep choice uses multiplication. Ordered arrangements and unordered selections require different formulas.

B. Why do we need it?

Probability ratios often require the number of possible outcomes. Binomial and hypergeometric probabilities also depend on counting how many arrangements or samples produce a given count.

C. Definitions

A factorial multiplies the positive integers up to its argument: 4! = 4×3×2×1 = 24; by definition 0! = 1. A permutation puts distinct objects into ordered positions. A combination selects a group without ordering its members. If swapping two selected people changes the outcome, order matters.

D. Formulas and conditions

Multistep total = n1 × n2 × ... × nk.
P(N, n) = N!/(N-n)!; C(N, n) = N!/[n!(N-n)!].

N is the number of distinct available objects; n is the number chosen, 0 ≤ n ≤ N. The permutation/combination formulas above assume no repeated use of an object. P(N, n) is the same quantity denoted P with superscript N and subscript n in L6. C(N, n), often printed as a stacked binomial coefficient, is pronounced "N choose n."

For n ordered positions with repetition allowed and N options each, the answer is Nn instead. Multiplication in counting is not an independence assumption about probabilities: it counts valid choices at each step. If the number of valid later choices depends on earlier choices, account for that dependence or split cases.

Do not equate "randomly select" with combination in every context; random selection may fill ordered positions. Repetition and replacement must be read explicitly. Simplify factorials before multiplying huge numbers. 0! is 1, so C(n,0) and C(n, n) both equal 1.

G9

Unions, intersections, complements and the addition rule

Source: L6 pp. 5-14; L7 pp. 1-6. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Event algebra translates everyday words into precise sets. "A or B" includes A alone, B alone and both; "A and B" contains only the overlap; "not A" includes everything outside A.

B. Why do we need it?

Many wrong MC options reverse OR and AND or forget to subtract overlap. Translating first makes the arithmetic almost mechanical.

C. Definitions

The union is A∪B, intersection A∩B and complement Ac. Mutually exclusive events have no common outcome: A∩B = ∅. This says they cannot happen together in the same experiment; it does not say they are independent. "A but not B" is A∩Bc. "Neither" is (A∪B)c. "Exactly one" consists of A only and B only.

D. Formulas and why they work

P(Ac) = 1-P(A); P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)-P(A∩B).

Adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once. If mutually exclusive, overlap = 0, and ordinary addition is enough. P(A only) = P(A)-P(both). P(neither) = 1-P(union). P(exactly one) = P(A)+P(B)-2P(both). Always ensure the four disjoint regions A only, both, B only and neither sum to 1.

De Morgan's laws: (A∪B)c = Ac∩Bc; (A∩B)c = Ac∪Bc. Negating "both" means at least one requirement fails, not necessarily both fail.

The remaining source identities (L6 pp. 7-8) are useful to recognize: union/intersection are commutative and associative; A∪A = A∩A = A; A∪∅ = A; A∩S = A; A∪Ac = S; A∩Ac = ∅. Absorption (called simplificative in the notes): A∪(A∩B) = A and A∩(A∪B) = A. Distributive: A∩(B∪C) = (A∩B)∪(A∩C), with the dual rule interchanging ∪ and ∩. These are statements about sets, so they need no independence assumption.

E. Guided source examples

1
L6 p. 6: fair die, A = odd = {1,3,5}, B = less than 5 = {1,2,3,4}. Union {1,2,3,4,5} has probability 5/6; intersection {1,3} has 2/6 = 1/3. Ac = {2,4,6}, Bc = {5,6}.
2
Thus A∩Bc = {5}, probability 1/6, while B∩Ac = {2,4}, probability 1/3.

L6 pp. 11-12: at least one head in five independent fair tosses. The complement is no heads, namely TTTTT. Its probability is (1/2)⁵ = 1/32. Therefore P(at least one) = 1-1/32 = 31/32 = 0.96875. The complement avoids listing 31 sequences.

G. Common mistakes

"At least one" includes multiple successes. Complementing "takes at least 5 courses AND lives on campus" gives "takes at most 4 courses OR does not live on campus." The count of courses is integer, so the negation of ≥5 is ≤4. Do not add overlapping categories without subtraction.

G10

Conditional probability and multiplication rules

Source: L7 pp. 7-17. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Conditional probability recalculates chance after some information is known. "Given B" restricts attention to B; outcomes outside B no longer belong in the denominator.

B. Why do we need it?

A randomly selected card has probability 13/52 of being a heart. If a heart has already been removed, the next-card calculation changes to 12 hearts among 51 cards. The information changes the relevant population of outcomes.

C. Definition

P(A|B) means probability of A given B, and is defined when P(B) > 0. The event after the bar determines the denominator. P(A|B) and P(B|A) generally differ.

D. Formulas and assumptions

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)Conditional probability: overlap divided by the probability of the given event.
P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B); P(A∩B) = P(B)P(A|B) = P(A)P(B|A).

The multiplication rule is conditional in general; independence is needed before replacing P(B|A) by P(B). For three sequential events: P(A∩B∩C) = P(A)P(B|A)P(C|A∩B), with each conditioning event of positive probability. In a tree, multiply along a path and add disjoint paths.

Conditional probabilities obey ordinary probability rules inside the restricted space: P(Ac|C) = 1-P(A|C); the union rule also applies with "given C" attached to every term. If A ⊆ C, P(A|C) = P(A)/P(C). If C ⊆ A and P(C)>0, then P(A|C) = 1. If A∩C is empty, it is zero. These are source properties from L7 p. 12.

G11

Independent versus mutually exclusive events

IndependentP(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). Learning one does not change the probability of the other.
Mutually exclusiveA∩B=∅. With both probabilities positive, these events are dependent.

Source: L8 pp. 1-9. Priority: Must Know; mutual independence extension If Time Permits.

A. What is it?

Events are independent when learning that one occurred does not change the other's probability. This is a probability relation, not a statement about whether events look related in ordinary language.

B. Why do we need it?

Independence justifies multiplying unconditional probabilities and is a required binomial assumption. Using it without checking can change an entire calculation.

C. Definition

A and B are independent exactly when P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B); otherwise they are dependent. If P(B)>0 this is equivalent to P(A|B) = P(A). Compare to the probability of A, not B. The analogous condition P(B|A) = P(B) requires P(A)>0.

D. Method and special properties

Compute the observed intersection and the product separately. Equality → independent; inequality → dependent.

Mutually exclusive means intersection is empty, so intersection probability is zero. If both probabilities are positive, their product is positive, so mutually exclusive events cannot be independent. Probability-zero and probability-one events are independent of every event under the product definition; conditional expressions with a zero denominator are still undefined.

Independence is preserved by complements: if A, B are independent, then Ac, B; A, Bc; Ac, Bc are also independent. For three mutually independent events, every pair must satisfy the product relation and the triple must satisfy P(A∩B∩C) = P(A)P(B)P(C). Pairwise independence alone is insufficient.

G12

Total probability and Bayes' theorem

Source: L8 pp. 10-19. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

Total probability combines several routes to an observed result. Bayes reverses the direction: after seeing the result, which route was responsible?

B. Why do we need it?

Different machines can have different production shares and defect rates. The overall defect probability must weight both. Once a defect is observed, the share of defects from a machine differs from its share of all production.

C. Definitions

A partition A1, ..., Am divides S into nonoverlapping groups that cover every outcome, with positive group probabilities in the source convention. A prior P(Ai) is the probability before the evidence. P(B|Ai) is the evidence probability within that group. P(Ai|B) is the posterior after evidence B is known.

D. Formulas and recognition clues

P(Aj|B)=P(Aj)P(B|Aj)P(B)Bayes: the selected evidence path divided by all evidence.
Total probability: P(B) = Σ P(Ai)P(B|Ai).
Bayes: P(Aj|B) = P(Aj)P(B|Aj) / [Σ P(Ai)P(B|Ai)], provided P(B) > 0.

The sum is over all groups in the partition. Each term is the joint probability of one group and B. Their disjointness permits addition. For just A and Ac, sum those two routes. "Overall, " "randomly chosen item is defective" signals total probability. "Given defective, from which machine?" signals Bayes. Bayes is conditional probability with the denominator expanded into all possible evidence routes.

E. Guided source example: screws

L8 pp. 12,16-17 gives M1 share 0.75, defect rate 0.04; M2 share 0.25, defect rate 0.02. Define D = defective. Need overall P(D), then P(M1|D).

1

2
For Bayes, numerator is the selected route, 0.030; denominator all defects, 0.035.
3
Thus P(M1|D) = 0.030/0.035 = 6/7 ≈ 0.8571.
4
P(M2|D) = 0.005/0.035 = 1/7 ≈ 0.1429. Posteriors sum to 1.

A frequency check makes this intuitive: among an imagined 10,000 screws, M1 makes 7500 with 300 defects; M2 makes 2500 with 50 defects. Of the total 350 defects, 300/350 = 6/7 come from M1. The imagined counts explain the probabilities; they are not extra observed data.

Do not average rates equally unless group sizes are equal. Do not divide by a prior instead of evidence probability. Include every evidence-producing route in the denominator. The Republican-letter sample question has an implicit random-opponent assumption; Document 4 distinguishes it from a literal letter-selection model.

G13

Discrete random variables, probability tables, expectation and variance

Source: L9 pp. 1-16; L10 pp. 1-6. Priority: Must Know.

A. What is it?

A random variable assigns one number to each outcome. Different outcomes can receive the same number. A distribution lists which values that number can take and how likely each is.

B. Why do we need it?

Instead of tracking every coin sequence, we may care only about winnings or number of heads. A probability table then lets us compute a long-run average and a measure of uncertainty.

C. Definitions

A discrete random variable has finitely many or countably many possible values. Those values may include negatives and decimals: the source's winnings -2,1.5,2.25 are discrete because there are just three possibilities. A continuous variable is modeled over a real interval, such as height or time. Counting cheeseburgers is discrete even if the count can be large.

p(x) means P(the random variable equals x). A valid table has nonnegative probabilities and total 1. Its support is the set of possible values with positive probability. Expected value µ = E(x) is the probability-weighted mean, interpreted as the long-run average under repeated trials of that model; it need not be a possible single outcome. Variance measures squared spread around µ. SD is its nonnegative square root.

D. Formulas and when to use them

σ2=E(x2)−[E(x)]2Variance is the second moment minus the square of the mean.
Σp(x) = 1; µ = E(x) = Σx p(x); E(x²) = Σx² p(x).
σ² = Var(x) = Σ(x-µ)²p(x) = E(x²)-µ²; σ = √σ².

Sums run over all possible values. x is a value, p(x) its weight, µ the mean, σ² the variance. There is no n-1 denominator: this is the complete theoretical distribution, not a sample-estimation formula. If a table contains k, use total probability 1 to solve k before calculating means or event probabilities.

For an event like -1 < x ≤ 5, list only support values satisfying both inequalities and add their masses. Never include values between support points that have no mass. E(x²) and [E(x)]² are different; the latter squares the mean. Variance is zero exactly when the variable equals its mean with probability 1 (a degenerate distribution).

Expectation properties from L9 p. 16: E(ax+b) = aE(x)+b and E(ax+by+c) = aE(x)+bE(y)+c, for fixed constants and finite expectations. Linearity does not require independence. If x, y are independent, E(xy) = E(x)E(y); without independence that product shortcut is not generally justified. Variance/SD are not interchangeable with expectation.

E. Guided source examples

1

G14

Discrete uniform, Bernoulli and binomial distributions

BinomialFixed n, independent binary trials, constant p.
HypergeometricUniform sampling without replacement from a finite population. See G15.

Source: L10 pp. 7-17. Priority: binomial Must Know; uniform/Bernoulli Should Know.

A. What is it?

Named distributions are reusable models. Uniform means all listed values are equally likely. Bernoulli records one success/failure trial as 1/0. Binomial counts successes across a fixed number of independent identical Bernoulli trials.

B. Why do we need it?

Enumerating 2²⁰ success/failure sequences is impractical. The binomial formula groups sequences with the same number of successes and computes their combined probability.

C. Definitions and recognition

Discrete uniform: m distinct values a1, ..., am each have probability 1/m. They need not be consecutive integers. Bernoulli: x=1 for success with probability p; x=0 for failure with probability q=1-p. Success is simply the event being counted; it need not be desirable.

Binomial checklist: fixed n trials; each trial has success/failure; trials independent; same probability p on every trial; x counts successes. If all hold, write x ~ Bin(n, p), meaning "x follows a binomial distribution with parameters n, p." If objects are drawn without replacement from a small finite group, p changes and binomial is generally not exact. See hypergeometric in G15.

D. Formulas, symbols and justification

P(x=k)=C(n,k)pk(1−p)n−kBinomial probability: count the arrangements, then multiply by one path probability.
Uniform: P(x=ai)=1/m; µ=(Σai)/m; σ²=(Σai²)/m-µ².
Bernoulli: µ=p; σ²=p(1-p); σ=√[p(1-p)].
Binomial: P(x=k)=C(n, k)pk qn-k, k=0,1, ..., n; q=1-p.
Binomial: µ=np; σ²=npq; σ=√(npq).

Here n = number of trials, k = requested success count, p = one-trial success probability, q = one-trial failure probability, and C(n, k)=n!/[k!(n-k)!] counts arrangements of k success positions. A particular sequence with k successes has probability pk qn-k, by independence. There are C(n, k) such disjoint sequences, so multiply. L10 pp. 14-15 derives this for n=2 and n=3; p. 16 generalizes it.

The binomial theorem (L10 p. 12) is (a+b)n = Σ C(n, k)ak bn-k. Taking a=p, b=q shows binomial probabilities sum to (p+q)n=1. You need not expand large powers by hand. If y=n-x counts failures, y ~ Bin(n, q), not Bin(n, p) unless p=q.

G15

Sample-exam prerequisites: Poisson and hypergeometric

Scope label: supplemental gap explanations. L10 p. 7 lists these names but supplies no full treatment. The original sample directly needs them at I10 and II10. The following short lessons are newly supplied mathematical prerequisites; they are not presented as missing lecture pages or assignments.

A. What are they?

Poisson models a count in a fixed interval with a stated average count. Hypergeometric models the number of designated successes in a fixed-size sample drawn uniformly without replacement from a finite population.

B. Why do we need them?

"0.8 accidents per day" is an average event count over time, not a one-trial success probability. "Select 10 from a class of 20" changes the remaining composition, so it is not automatically binomial.

C. Core definitions and assumptions

For Poisson, λ (lambda) is the expected count for the specified interval, λ>0. When using the model from a process description, a roughly constant rate and independent counts in disjoint intervals are typical assumptions; the sample explicitly states the model, so you need not prove it from traffic data. If the rate is per day and the question concerns two days, adjust the mean to two days under the stated constant-rate model.

For hypergeometric, N = population size, r = number of objects in the success category, n = sample size, x = number of successes selected. These parameters depend on what x counts. The possible integer x range is max(0, n-(N-r)) through min(n, r). This simply prevents choosing more successes or failures than exist.

D. Minimum useful formulas

Poisson: P(x=k)=e-λλk/k!, k=0,1,2, ...; P(x=0)=e-λ; µ=σ²=λ.
Hypergeometric: P(x=k)=C(r, k)C(N-r, n-k)/C(N, n).

e≈2.71828 is a mathematical constant; use the calculator's exponential function for e raised to a power. In the hypergeometric formula, choose k from r successes and n-k from the failures, then divide by all possible samples. For recognition, the essential fact is finite uniform sampling without replacement and the correct success category. Its mean is n(r/N); the full variance formula is not needed to resolve the supplied sample.

I1. Reliability and including the entire population · Show answer & full solution

Original p. 2. Answer: True in the course's intended sampling context. Level: easy recognition, with a wording qualification. Source: L1 pp. 7-9; guide G1.

The statement says inference requires a measure of reliability and claims certainty requires the whole population. A sample can differ from its population, so an inference needs an account of uncertainty. Including every population member is a census, removing sampling error. This is the intended idea repeated in L1 p. 8. A census still does not guarantee freedom from measurement, recording or nonresponse errors; the statement is too broad if read as a claim about every kind of error.

Trap: interpreting representative sampling as absolute certainty. New variation: decide whether an error-free census has sampling uncertainty, distinguishing that from data-entry mistakes.

4. Source corrections and conventions

These are the main issues relevant to solving questions. They distinguish definite errors from conventions and assumptions.

L2 p. 25, left-skew example - definite arithmetic error. The source displays 2601/5 = 502.2 for 1, 500, 600, 700, 800. The sum 2601 is correct; division gives 520.2. It is still below median 600, so the intended comparison remains valid. The mean-tail relationship is a useful pattern, not a theorem for every possible skewness definition or irregular dataset.

L3 p. 14, Chebyshev 95% row - numerical error. The row uses k = 4.72 with 0.95. At k = 4.72, 1-1/k² ≈ 0.9551, not 0.9500. Solving 1-1/k² = 0.95 gives k = √20 ≈ 4.4721. Calling the 4.72 bound at least 95% is conservative, but the table's equality is inaccurate.

L3 p. 16, inverse Chebyshev interval - unstated assumption. The source gives [61,83] with k = 2.3, mean 72 and SD 5. Direct endpoints are 72 ± 11.5, namely 60.5 and 83.5. The listed integer interval [61,83] describes the same included integer marks if whole-number marks are assumed. For arbitrary real marks use the unrounded endpoints. The exact inverse for 81.1% is k = 1/√0.189 ≈ 2.3002, close to the source's rounded 2.3.

L3 p. 19 and L4 p. 10 - rounding differences, not substantive contradictions. Diagrams use about 68.3% and 95.4%; text uses 68% and 95%. In this course's empirical-rule questions use 68/95/99.7 unless more precise normal-table values are specifically requested. Do not mix levels of approximation within one calculation.

L4 p. 3 and pp. 5-6 - percentile convention ambiguity. The definition permits either simple or weighted averages for noninteger r. The worked 85th percentile uses r = 13.6 and (96+98)/2 = 97. Weighted interpolation gives 97.2. This package follows the demonstrated simple average of adjacent ranks and says so explicitly. Neither method is universally the only valid percentile convention. Very extreme ranks outside 1 through n are not specified by these notes; no invented endpoint rule is used.

L4 pp. 16-20 - preserve course outlier labels. The prose and pictures are less precise at exact fences than p. 20's inequalities. Use p. 20: distance from the nearer quartile divided by IQR from 1.5 through 3 inclusive means potential outlier; greater than 3 means outlier. For z, 2 ≤ |z| ≤ 3 means potential and |z| > 3 means outlier. Many textbooks call all beyond-inner-fence points outliers; that is a terminology difference. State the rule used. The boxplot image on p. 17 has confusing min/max annotations; use the p. 16 prose: whiskers terminate at actual observed values inside the inner fences, not automatically at the numerical fences.

L10 p. 4 - definite conflicting arithmetic. Method 1 prints E(x²) = 3.820125, variance 3.81621875, SD about 1.9535. For x = -2, 1.5, 2.25 with probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.25, direct computation gives E(x) = -0.0625; E(x²) = 2+0.5625+1.265625 = 3.828125; variance = 3.82421875; SD ≈ 1.955561. Method 2's values are correct. The missing decimal in Method 1's squared mean is also a typographical problem.

L10 p. 2 - units error. The source says variance is often unitless. For a variable measured in dollars, variance has dollars², while SD has dollars. Variance is unitless only when the variable itself is unitless (or appropriately standardized).

L1 pp. 8,14 and sample I1 - simplified wording. A census removes sampling error for that population; it does not eliminate recording, measurement or nonresponse errors. A simple random sample gives every same-size subset equal probability; it does not guarantee that a particular realized sample perfectly matches every population characteristic. The sample I1 intended answer is True in its sampling-uncertainty context, with this qualification.

Sample I10 - parameter convention and implicit random selection. There are 8 girls, not 12. When x counts selected girls, the success-category count is r = 8 under the sample's intended hypergeometric notation. The supplied r = 12 counts boys; intended answer False. Some texts use a differently named parameter, so always define success. Uniform random selection without replacement is the necessary assumption.

Sample III2(b) - modeling qualification. The intended answer treats the letter writer as a randomly chosen opposing voter. Letter-writing probabilities by party are not supplied. Thus Bayes gives the intended result under this assumption; an actual newspaper-letter process with party-dependent writing rates would be underdetermined.
